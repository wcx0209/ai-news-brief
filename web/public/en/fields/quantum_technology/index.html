Story angle

Finding faster quantum algorithms with agents and formal verification

Google DeepMind
Source published 2026-11-24 00:59 SGT
Official release
AI × Energy

According to Google DeepMind: Google researchers report that teams of Gemini 4 AI agents, guided by formal theorem proving in Lean, successfully optimized quantum algorithms for fields like nuclear fusion and drug discovery. The method reduced resource costs significantly across several benchmark problems, and the team has open-sourced their Lean library and verified solutions on GitHub.

Expand summary

According to Google DeepMind: Researchers from Google DeepMind and Google Quantum AI report using teams of Gemini 4 agents to improve quantum algorithms, creating highly efficient quantum circuits for complex scientific challenges like fusion energy and drug design. Because early resource-intensive quantum circuits cannot be tested on classical computers or current hardware, the team used Lean, a formal theorem prover, to mathematically verify every agent-generated result. Independent agent teams competed and traded ideas in virtual conferences every four hours, gradually reducing computational costs. According to the paper, the agents reduced computing costs for inertial confinement fusion by 11.8 times and lowered memory requirements. For drug metabolism and photochemical reactions, the agents achieved an 8 times cost reduction for light-sensitive molecules and a 2.5 times improvement for Cytochrome P450. Furthermore, they found a 2.57 times speedup for Grover's algorithm and a 290 times speedup for an arcsin arithmetic routine. The researchers are open-sourcing their Lean library, problem specifications, and verified solutions on GitHub to help the broader community build upon this work.

Global First Real-Machine Implementation of Quantum Computer High-Speed Memory

TMTPost 钛媒体
Source published 2026-11-23 07:20 SGT
Media report

Researchers in China have achieved the world's first real-machine implementation of quantum random access memory (QRAM) using a superconducting quantum chip. This breakthrough functions similarly to installing high-speed memory in a quantum computer, and the related findings have been published in *Nature Physics*.

Expand summary

At the superconducting quantum computing laboratory in Zhejiang University, a superconducting quantum chip named 'Tianmu-2' successfully executed the global first real-machine implementation of QRAM (Quantum Random Access Memory). This collaborative research involved multiple teams from Zhejiang University's School of Physics, School of Computer Science and Technology, College of Software, and the Ningbo International Science and Technology Innovation Center.

As a crucial component for bridging quantum processors and classical or quantum data storage, QRAM has long remained a theoretical challenge in the field. This experimental realization overcomes previous technical bottlenecks, effectively providing quantum computers with an equivalent to high-speed memory and significantly enhancing future data-processing potential. The study has been officially published in the international journal *Nature Physics*, marking a solid step forward for practical quantum computing hardware development.

QuEra joins 12 million dollar US project to use AI for quantum error correction

The Quantum Insider
Source published 2026-11-24 02:39 SGT
Media report
AI

QuEra Computing has joined a Harvard-led, 12 million dollar U.S. Department of Energy project to develop AI-designed error correction for quantum computers. The ASQC project brings together national labs, universities, NVIDIA, and Anthropic to tailor error-correcting codes to specific scientific workloads. Researchers will test these systems on QuEra's neutral-atom quantum computers, with the ultimate goal of reducing the overhead that slows down fault-tolerant quantum computing.

Expand summary

According to QuEra Computing, the company has partnered in the Application-Aware Error Correction Codesign for Scientific Quantum Computing project, known as ASQC. This initiative is part of the Department of Energy's Genesis Mission and aims to leverage AI models to identify the errors that most heavily impact scientific workloads like chemistry, materials science, and nuclear science. By designing tailored error-correcting codes and decoders, the project seeks to lower the heavy resource overhead that currently separates noisy hardware from fault-tolerant quantum computers.

The project team includes prominent researchers from Harvard, MIT, Cornell, and national laboratories alongside industry partners like NVIDIA and Anthropic. According to QuEra, the effort builds on prior work, including a preprint describing a neural-network decoder that reduced error rates, and previous tests where large language models like Claude helped control hardware testbeds. QuEra CEO Andy Ory states that application-co-designed error correction could accelerate the company's roadmap toward its planned Libra fault-tolerant system on AWS in early 2028, though commercial deployment timelines remain subject to ongoing hardware and software development.
