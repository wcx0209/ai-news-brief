Raise3D Launches T450 and T700 FDM 3D Printers Alongside New Software and Post-Processing Hardware

TCT Magazine
Source published 2026-11-13 18:17 SGT
Media report

Raise3D has announced the T450 and T700 FDM 3D printing systems featuring large build volumes and heated chambers. According to the company, these machines utilize CoreXY motion and TwinFlow extrusion technology to achieve high printing speeds, alongside updates to existing SLS printers and a new automated sandblaster.

Expand summary

Product Launch Details

Raise3D has introduced the T450 and large-format T700 FDM 3D printers ahead of Formnext 2026. Both models share a CoreXY motion platform with four frame-mounted motors and TwinFlow high-flow extrusion technology, which feeds two filament paths into a single nozzle. The systems include Air Flow Manager 2 to adjust chamber temperatures up to 70°C, and feature fiber-ready nozzles reaching 420°C.

Applications and Pricing

According to CEO Edward Feng, the T450's build area enables applications like single-piece adolescent scoliosis braces. Raise3D also reported significant production time and weight reductions for battery cell transport trays. The T450 is priced at $4,999, with deliveries starting in December 2026, while the T700 is priced at $15,999, with deliveries expected by January 2027.

Additional Hardware and Software

Alongside the new printers, Raise3D announced a 100 mm build-height increase for its RMS220 SLS printer via a software update, and introduced the B520 automated sandblaster for polymer powder bed parts. These developments follow a recent $15 million funding round aimed at supporting product portfolio upgrades and market expansion.

MIT and Northeastern engineers design stronger, more resilient metamaterials inspired by nature

MIT Advanced Manufacturing
Source published 2026-11-12 00:00 SGT
Official release
Materials

According to MIT Advanced Manufacturing: Researchers have created a new type of metamaterial by introducing controlled disorder inspired by natural networks like leaf veins and dragonfly wings. Unlike traditional materials built from repeating identical blocks, these new materials use varying sizes of beams to distribute damage and absorb impacts much better. This breakthrough could lead to safer helmets, stronger armor, and advanced acoustic equipment.

Expand summary

According to MIT Advanced Manufacturing:

Background

For decades, synthetic metamaterials have been designed using a single building block, or unit cell, copied and pasted to form a precise, periodic microstructure. While this ordered approach improves overall performance, it has a major flaw: if one region cracks, the surrounding identical cells quickly buckle, leading to catastrophic failure.

Details

To overcome this limitation, a team from MIT and Northeastern University turned to network science, a field traditionally used to study complex systems like the internet or neural connections. Using a specialized algorithm, the researchers generated a physical network of intersecting beams with varying sizes and thicknesses, mimicking the web-like structures found in nature. By 3D-printing these patterns into polymer samples, they created scale-rich metamaterials that avoid the localization effect seen in ordered structures.

Impact

Physical tests showed that these network-inspired materials offer a widely controllable range of stiffness, resist sudden impacts more effectively, and can successfully cushion fragile objects. This design framework opens the door to next-generation protective gear, impact-absorbing structures, and specialized materials that control vibrations.
