Riton releases M-100P micro metal 3D printer with 10-micron laser spot

Nanjixiong 南极熊3D打印
Source published 2026-11-17 00:00 SGT
Media report

According to Nanjixiong, Riton 3D has launched the M-100P, an ultra-fine metal 3D printer featuring a 10-micron laser spot and a +/- 2-micron repetition accuracy. This system aims to break traditional accuracy limits for micro-structures in medical and precision electronics by using direct selective laser melting.

Expand summary

According to Nanjixiong, Riton 3D released the M-100P micro metal 3D printer on November 17, 2026, pushing selective laser melting into a micrometer-scale territory.

Core Technology

Equipped with a self-developed ultra-fine laser and optical system, the M-100P achieves a 10-micron laser spot, a 15-micron melt track width, and a +/- 2-micron repetition accuracy. Its build volume is 100 mm in diameter by 80 mm in height. By combining closed-loop slicing algorithms with high-speed galvanometer scanning, the company reports that the printing speed is twice as fast as comparable systems, while yield strength and tensile strength increase by 15% to 25% due to finer grain structures.

Applications and Impact

The equipment supports stainless steel, titanium, and cobalt-chromium alloys, targeting high-value applications such as structural heart stents, medical puncture needles, and micro-electronic components. By reducing surface roughness and minimizing spatter, it bypasses heavy post-processing, offering an efficient alternative to traditional high-cost micro-machining.

Why Britain struggles to manufacture the future it invents

3D Printing Industry
Source published 2026-11-17 01:39 SGT
Media report

Ahead of Formnext 2026, industry reports indicate that while the UK excels at 3D printing research and patents, it struggles to scale domestic production and retain ownership. Founders note that public grants often fund initial engineering rather than business growth, while a small local customer base forces companies to look overseas for sustainable demand.

Expand summary

The Innovation Paradox

Britain has a long history of industrial pioneering and remains a global hub for additive manufacturing research, backed by major academic institutes. However, statistics show a gap between invention and local commercial production. While British researchers generate thousands of patents, much of the actual manufacturing and component supply often shifts overseas, leaving local machinists and startups fighting for steady domestic orders.

Funding and Commercialization

Founders of UK-based 3D printing startups report that while government innovation grants help develop early technology, they fail to support the broader commercialization process. Turning a working prototype into a reliable product requires solving training, maintenance, and customer validation issues, which demands capital that grants rarely cover. Additionally, university spin-outs historically faced heavy equity dilution, though recent data shows universities are taking smaller stakes to help founders raise money.

The Demand Signal

Industry leaders emphasize that the core issue is not a lack of engineering talent, but a shortage of local customers willing to install industrial equipment and buy production parts at scale. Without a robust domestic installed base, companies face higher training costs and often look to international markets, particularly the US, for growth. Experts suggest that clearer government procurement signals and stronger public-private co-investment could help bridge this gap.
