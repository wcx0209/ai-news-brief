New approach enables physically consistent AI models of chaotic systems

MIT Advanced Manufacturing
Source published 2026-11-21 04:00 SGT
Official release
AI

According to MIT Advanced Manufacturing: Researchers at MIT have developed a new method to add physics-based hard constraints to machine learning models, ensuring they do not drift into impossible predictions over time. Published in *PNAS*, this approach uses energy-based restrictions to keep chaotic systems like weather and fluid dynamics within realistic bounds, offering a reliable way to improve AI reliability in high-stakes fields.

Expand summary

According to MIT Advanced Manufacturing:

Background

Predicting complex and chaotic systems like weather patterns, ocean currents, and turbulent flows is notoriously difficult. While artificial intelligence models can learn from data, they often suffer from a fundamental flaw: small initial errors accumulate over time, causing simulations to drift into physically impossible states where values grow without limit.

Details

According to MIT researchers Sunbochen Tang, Navid Azizan, and Themistoklis Sapsis, previous attempts relied on soft penalties during training, which offered no guarantee at test time. The team’s new approach introduces a projection layer that enforces hard constraints based on real-world energy dissipation. The model learns an energy-like function and a confinement boundary, ensuring that predictions remain bounded over arbitrarily long forecast windows while still preserving complex chaotic behaviors.

Impact

Tested across various physical systems, the modular and model-agnostic approach closely matched true outcomes compared to conventional methods. The team has made their code and paper freely available online, paving the way for more trustworthy and reliable AI applications in meteorology, aviation, and industrial processes.

Fraunhofer IAPT uses ultrasound to remove 3D metal printing supports

3D Printing Industry
Source published 2026-11-21 17:18 SGT
Media report

German research institute Fraunhofer IAPT has developed an automated process that uses ultrasound to remove support structures from 3D printed metal parts. According to the institute, the method takes only a few minutes and works without manual labor, solving a major bottleneck in metal additive manufacturing.

Expand summary

Background and Process

Removing support structures is traditionally one of the most labor-intensive and hazardous steps in metal additive manufacturing, often exposing workers to fine metal powder. To tackle this, Fraunhofer IAPT has created an ultrasonic process that applies high-frequency mechanical vibrations to printed components. By pairing custom-designed support structures with tailored vibration settings, the method concentrates stress where the supports attach, causing them to break away cleanly within a few minutes.

Application and Industry Impact

Originating from the Sonodent research project, the technology is initially being applied in dental technology but is designed to scale across other industries. While Fraunhofer IAPT focuses on ultrasonic vibrations, other players are tackling the same bottleneck through robotics, such as Rivelin Robotics, or redesigned breakaway structures, like those from SnowX, highlighting a broader industry push toward automated post-processing.

AI is evolving fast. Here is how Siemens plans to help data centers keep up

Siemens Blog
Source published 2026-11-20 15:49 SGT
Official release
AI

According to Siemens Blog: AI technologies are developing rapidly, but building traditional data centers still takes years. Siemens executives at NVIDIA GTC Berlin 2026 report that closing this gap requires digital twin simulation, modular construction, and closer collaboration between IT and power systems.

Expand summary

According to Siemens Blog:

At NVIDIA GTC Berlin 2026, Siemens global solutions director Diego Witte and US data center head John DeBoer outlined the major hurdles facing AI infrastructure. While AI chips and compute demands scale rapidly, data centers face bottlenecks in scale, speed, skilled labor, and power availability.

To overcome these issues, Siemens advocates a shift toward digital twin simulation to test liquid cooling and rack configurations before physical construction. They also stress the need for standardized reference designs to align compute, power, and cooling ecosystems, and modular construction to combat labor shortages.

Furthermore, the report emphasizes that data centers must evolve into active grid participants. By combining software automation with physical hardware like battery storage and supercapacitors, and integrating IT with operational technology (OT), facilities can better manage extreme energy demands and improve overall efficiency.
