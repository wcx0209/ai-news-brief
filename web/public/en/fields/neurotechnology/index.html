Brain-computer interface lets paralyzed patients control smart home devices using silent speech

Nature Brain-Machine Interfaces
Source published 2026-11-11 08:00 SGT
Research paper
Text report · No source image available

A new brain-computer interface allows a person with ALS to control smart home devices simply by silently mouthing words, without needing any visual or auditory prompts. In clinical tests, this system achieved a high decoding accuracy of over 97 percent, proving that brain signals from internal speech can be reliably used for private and independent device control.

Expand summary

Background

Brain-computer interfaces hold great promise for restoring independence to individuals with paralysis, enabling them to interact with their environment and operate smart devices. However, previous BCI systems for device control often relied on external visual displays or audio cues, which restricted the user's ability to issue commands autonomously and privately in everyday settings.

Details

In this recent study, a clinical trial participant living with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis used a chronically implanted electrocorticographic brain-computer interface. Over the course of 18 experimental sessions, the system detected silently mimed speech commands in real time. The technology successfully decoded these internal signals with a median accuracy of 97.1 percent, achieving nearly 12 correct decodes per minute without relying on any exogenous timing cues.

Impact

These findings represent a major step forward for assistive technology, demonstrating that silently attempted speech can be translated reliably into actionable commands. This capability significantly improves both the privacy and autonomy of people with severe motor impairments, bringing practical, hands-free smart home control one step closer to reality.

Can AI Foundation Models Actually Explain How the Brain Works?

The Transmitter
Source published 2026-11-12 02:44 SGT
Media report
AI

Tech giants and academic labs are pouring millions into training AI foundation models on massive amounts of brain data. While these models get good at predicting brain activity, top scientists are locked in a debate over whether accurate predictions actually mean we understand the brain, or if we are just building another black box that we still have to figure out.

Expand summary

The Brain Data Rush

Researchers are applying the recipe behind large language models to neuroscience, training neural networks on vast brain recordings. Meta, Stanford, and startups have released or are building brain foundation models, hoping that scale will make them smarter.

The Great Debate

Optimists argue that models reproducing brain activity can act as stand-ins for living subjects and help us discover new biological insights. Skeptics counter that fitting brain data does not guarantee the model works like a real brain, nor does it grant mechanistic understanding. Scaling laws for brain data also remain murky compared to language models.

The Path Forward

Some suggest combining data-driven models with task-trained models, while others argue that true intelligence requires modeling the whole body and physical interactions, reminding the field that scaling data might not be a silver bullet.
