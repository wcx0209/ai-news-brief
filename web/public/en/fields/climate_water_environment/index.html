Bill Gates-backed Breakthrough Energy says global decarbonization would cost 7.5 trillion dollars

MIT Climate
Source published 2026-11-13 05:30 SGT
Official release
AI

According to MIT Climate: At an MIT energy conference, Breakthrough Energy CTO Eric Toone reported that global decarbonization would cost roughly 7.5 trillion dollars, or 5 percent of world GDP. Utilizing artificial intelligence to analyze costs, the firm emphasizes that success relies heavily on eliminating the green premium because people are generally unwilling to pay extra for eco-friendly alternatives.

Expand summary

According to MIT Climate:

Background and Costs

Eric Toone, chief technical officer at Breakthrough Energy, spoke at MIT's annual energy conference, highlighting that rising global prosperity inherently drives up energy demand and greenhouse gas emissions. According to Toone, while decarbonizing the world economy is entirely feasible, the true challenge lies not just in the calculated 7.5 trillion dollar cost, but in the reality that consumers and even wealthy individuals are largely unwilling to pay extra for zero-carbon alternatives. By enlisting artificial intelligence, the organization estimated this multi-trillion-dollar price tag, which could potentially drop to 3.5 trillion dollars if emerging technologies succeed.

Technology Solutions

Breakthrough Energy Ventures has deployed approximately 3 billion dollars into roughly 140 companies tackling clean energy challenges. Noteworthy examples include Luxwall, which produces highly insulating windows capable of cutting commercial building energy use by 30 percent, and Form Energy, which is developing iron-air batteries designed to store power for up to a month. Other portfolio companies are addressing cement emissions, upgrading transmission lines, advancing geothermal drilling through adapted fracking technology, and creating magnetic containment solutions for fusion energy.

Industry Impact

Toone noted that while Breakthrough's investments are substantial, they represent only a fraction of broader global clean tech funding. The ultimate hurdle facing the energy transition is the sheer scale of existing infrastructure that must be replaced, making it critical to deploy these innovative technologies directly into emerging and developing economies to prevent future carbon-heavy infrastructure buildouts.

MIT researchers develop a new training method for faster and more trustworthy climate AI models

MIT Climate
Source published 2026-11-12 13:00 SGT
Official release
AI

According to MIT Climate: MIT researchers have created a new training technique that dramatically speeds up the production of climate emulators, which are AI models used to simulate future climate scenarios. By using optimized synthetic data instead of traditional realistic emission trajectories, the method cuts down data requirements and helps the AI adapt to unfamiliar scenarios that traditional models usually fail to handle.

Expand summary

According to MIT Climate:

Traditional earth system models used for climate projection are extremely powerful, but they take months to run on supercomputers for just a single scenario. To solve this, scientists use climate emulators, which are fast AI models that mimic full-scale systems. However, training these emulators usually requires running complex models on six to eight realistic future emission scenarios, making the data creation process slow and costly.

According to MIT researchers, emulators trained exclusively on realistic scenarios often fail to adapt when faced with unfamiliar or novel situations. To address this, the team developed an algorithm that generates a synthetic dataset of optimized, non-traditional climate scenarios tailored to boost predictive performance. By introducing rapid variations and high-frequency oscillations into the training data, the algorithm allows the emulator to extract much more information about the overall system while requiring far fewer full-scale model runs.

The resulting emulators proved capable of accurately predicting outcomes in scenarios they had never seen during training, such as local pollutant control policies where some emissions rise while others fall. Published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, this technique aims to reduce the months or years typically spent on emulator production, helping researchers and decision-makers get updated climate tools much faster.
