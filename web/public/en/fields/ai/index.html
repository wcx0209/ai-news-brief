LLMON: An LLM-Native Markup Language, now in Granite 4.1 3B and 8B

Hugging Face
Source published 2026-11-13 03:07 SGT
Media report

Researchers have developed LLMON, an LLM-native markup language designed to explicitly communicate structure and semantics to models, bypassing the limitations of traditional JSON. By separating instructions from data using reserved special tokens, the newly released experimental Granite 4.1 LLMON preview models achieve significantly higher resistance to prompt injection attacks while maintaining general capabilities.

Expand summary

Background and Motivation

Large language models rely heavily on context to generate tokens, but traditional formats like JSON were designed for parsers rather than causal models. Because transformers struggle with long spans and statistical quotation matching, models can become brittle or vulnerable to attacks such as prompt injections. To fix this, developers created LLMON (LLM Object Notation), which uses an XML-like structure with reserved special tokens to cleanly separate instructions, data, and requirements.

Implementation and Capabilities

Today, experimental preview models derived from Granite 4.1 base models, specifically granite-4.1-3b-LLMON-preview and granite-4.1-8b-LLMON-preview, have been released alongside a GitHub toolkit. Unlike regular chat templates that flatten inputs into a single string, LLMON wraps semantically coherent components in meaningful tags and uses an execution block to bind specific instructions to their exact data payloads. Evaluated on the OPI benchmark, these models virtually eliminate static indirect prompt injections, showing significantly higher robustness compared to standard open-source and closed-source models while matching general capabilities.

Zugwise: Domain-Aware Game Reconstruction for Digitizing Handwritten Chess Scoresheets

arXiv Artificial Intelligence
Source published 2026-11-13 13:00 SGT
Preprint

The authors report in this preprint: Researchers present Zugwise, a domain-aware system that treats handwritten chess scoresheet digitization as game reconstruction rather than isolated handwriting recognition, using computer-chess machinery and human-move priors to catch silent errors.

Expand summary

The authors report in this preprint: Digitizing handwritten chess scoresheets has traditionally been framed as a handwriting-recognition task combined with move legality checks. However, the authors argue that this misses silent errors where a misread yields a legal but incorrect move, corrupting the remainder of the game. To address this, the authors present Zugwise, which repurposes computer-chess machinery—such as quiescence search, static exchange evaluation, and pin detection—to evaluate whether a human could have plausibly written a given sequence. It incorporates an absurdity detector, backward search for tracing error symptoms, and a Needleman-Wunsch merge combining evidence from both players' sheets. Evaluated on tournament sections in an oracle replay, the system reduced silent errors from 16 to 1 and from 50 to 13. Two learned signals (CTC re-scoring and a Maia-style human-move prior) raised the correct fix's top-rank rate from 58.8% to 78.0%. The software runs entirely in the browser and is free, private, and offline-capable, accompanied by a three-way adjudication protocol.

Abstention Is Not Threshold Compliance: Stated Scoring Rules in Open Language Models

arXiv Computation and Language
Source published 2026-11-13 13:00 SGT
Preprint

The authors report in this preprint: Researchers investigate whether open language models can follow explicitly stated scoring rules with probability thresholds for abstention. They find that while formatting prompts to abstain can increase abstention rates, models fail to adjust their decisions correctly when the numerical threshold changes, indicating a lack of true threshold compliance.

Expand summary

The authors report in this preprint: This paper investigates the proposal by Kalai et al. to use explicit probability thresholds in prompts as scoring rules, directing language models to answer only when their confidence exceeds threshold t. The authors probe 12 checkpoints across three post-training stages from four open 3-8B model recipes, alongside exploratory models up to 32B. The empirical tests reveal that while a strict abstention format line shifts mean abstention from 20% to 91% at t = 0.75, varying t from 0.5 to 0.9 within this format changes abstention by a mere -0.8 percentage points. Without formatting prompts, higher thresholds can paradoxically lower abstention, with up to 40% of items receiving decisions incompatible with threshold-independent beliefs. Furthermore, external thresholds on recalibrated P(True) showed brittle generalization across datasets like TriviaQA and PopQA, and models trained on threshold-conditioned targets failed to track unseen thresholds consistently. The study concludes that high abstention rates do not imply true rule compliance and outlines rigorous evaluation criteria for stated scoring rules.

Expand summary

Background and Survey Data

Survey data indicate that generative AI users in China will exceed 700 million by mid-2026, with about a third using the tools for work. Workers use paid AI tools across four main clusters: drafting and slides, meeting notes and translation, data cleaning and light coding, and specialized work like contract review. Pricing design opacity and token-based metering drive up monthly expenses, as users often stack multiple subscriptions and burn credits on prompt tuning. Until employers treat AI access as standard infrastructure or vendors make pricing more transparent, workers will continue to subsidize the efficiency expected by their employers.

Building strong safety evaluations

Anthropic Research
Source published 2026-11-13 08:00 SGT
Official release

According to Anthropic Research: Anthropic engineering team shares guidelines for building reliable AI safety evaluations, drawing on psychology reforms and internal practices. The post emphasizes that safety claims depend entirely on robust test design, accurate constructs like appropriate responses rather than mere refusals, multi-turn simulations, rigorous grader validation, and preregistration to prevent gaming and noise in AI assessments.

Expand summary

According to Anthropic Research:

Background and Challenges

Safety evaluations guide critical decisions regarding AI model deployment and safeguards. However, researchers have found that AI evals are easily gamed, poorly documented, and sensitive to minor formatting changes. Drawing on psychological research reforms, Anthropic outlines best practices for building trustworthy safety evaluations.

Design and Execution Best Practices

Building strong evaluations requires matching the design to the core question, whether observational or causal, and measuring the right construct, such as appropriate response rates instead of simple refusal rates in sensitive contexts like self-harm or dual-use biology. Evals should use multi-turn simulations to capture nuanced, escalating user behavior and run in secure, sandboxed environments to prevent unintended actions.

Grader Validation and Reporting

Since modern evaluations rely on AI graders, teams must calibrate them rigorously, ensure atomic and binary criteria, and validate them against human labels. Furthermore, researchers should preregister analysis plans, report full statistics with confidence intervals, and disclose model configurations transparently to ensure results are reproducible.
