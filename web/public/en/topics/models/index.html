AlphaGenome 2 predicts how DNA changes affect specific human cells

Google DeepMindBiotech & Programmable LifeModelsSource published2026-11-11 23:00 SGT
Official releaseStage not established

According to Google DeepMind: AlphaGenome 2 is a next-generation DNA sequence model designed to bridge the gap between single-cell biology and human genetics. Unlike the original version which analyzed broad tissues in parallel, AlphaGenome 2 uses a conditional architecture to evaluate how a million-letter DNA sequence behaves in one specific cell type or state at a time. Trained on over 107 million individual cell profiles from humans and mice, the model can predict gene expression, chromatin accessibility, and DNA methylation across specialized cells. It can also generalize to unseen cell states, such as those triggered by diseases or vaccinations, without requiring retraining. In benchmark tests, AlphaGenome 2 successfully identified nearly twice as many causal variants and directional gene effects compared to previous tools. It has already demonstrated the ability to pinpoint complex disease drivers, such as identifying a regulatory variant that overactivates the ETS2 gene in gut immune cells to cause inflammatory bowel disease. The tool is now available for non-commercial research via an API, with commercial access offered in private preview, and future integration planned for Google's agentic development platform.

Training Methodology The model undergoes a four-stage post-training process. HOPE: A Smarter Way to Trim Large AI Models Without Losing Brainpower

Hugging FaceArtificial IntelligenceModelsSource published2026-11-09 20:34 SGT
Media reportStage not established

Background Large Language Models increasingly rely on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures to scale up to hundreds of billions of parameters without burning excessive compute during inference. However, a major bottleneck is that every expert must be loaded into memory, even though only a tiny fraction are active for any given token. For instance, in models like Qwen3.5, expert layers consume roughly 95% of the total parameter memory footprint. Details To fix this, researchers use expert pruning to permanently delete the least useful experts. While older methods evaluate each expert independently, the creators of HOPE realized that experts form cooperative groups that activate together. HOPE introduces a second-order method utilizing a quadratic program to calculate pairwise expert interactions. This ensures that when an expert is removed, the interdependent cluster remains intact. Impact In tests across models like Qwen and GLM, HOPE excelled at aggressive pruning rates. At a 50% pruning rate, HOPE achieved top performance, matching the unpruned model's score on agentic coding benchmarks like SWE-Bench Pro, whereas traditional pruning methods suffered significant performance drops. This allows for massive memory savings, leaving room for longer contexts and deeper reasoning without hardware upgrades.
