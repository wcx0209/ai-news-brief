AlphaGenome 2 predicts how DNA changes affect specific human cells

Google DeepMindBiotech & Programmable LifeModelsSource published2026-11-11 23:00 SGT
Official releaseStage not established

According to Google DeepMind: AlphaGenome 2 is a next-generation DNA sequence model designed to bridge the gap between single-cell biology and human genetics. Unlike the original version which analyzed broad tissues in parallel, AlphaGenome 2 uses a conditional architecture to evaluate how a million-letter DNA sequence behaves in one specific cell type or state at a time. Trained on over 107 million individual cell profiles from humans and mice, the model can predict gene expression, chromatin accessibility, and DNA methylation across specialized cells. It can also generalize to unseen cell states, such as those triggered by diseases or vaccinations, without requiring retraining. In benchmark tests, AlphaGenome 2 successfully identified nearly twice as many causal variants and directional gene effects compared to previous tools. It has already demonstrated the ability to pinpoint complex disease drivers, such as identifying a regulatory variant that overactivates the ETS2 gene in gut immune cells to cause inflammatory bowel disease. The tool is now available for non-commercial research via an API, with commercial access offered in private preview, and future integration planned for Google's agentic development platform.

FDA's new rare disease framework faces its first big test with Capricor's DMD drug

BioSpace Drug DevelopmentBiotech & Programmable LifeProgrammable LifeSource published2026-11-11 03:06 SGT
Media reportStage not established

Capricor Therapeutics is seeking FDA approval for deramiocel, a cell therapy designed to preserve upper limb function in non-ambulatory Duchenne muscular dystrophy patients. In its Phase 3 trial, the drug slowed upper limb decline by 54% compared to placebo, though the statistical analysis plan was revised, sparking debate and scrutiny from FDA reviewers during an advisory committee meeting. The debate underscores the core dilemma of the FDA's new Rare Disease Framework Initiative. In small patient populations with progressive diseases, running large trials is nearly impossible, and missing data is common. Experts argue that the FDA needs to explicitly define how it handles analysis plan changes, evaluates the totality of evidence, and values slowing functional loss as a meaningful clinical outcome. The upcoming decision will reveal whether the FDA's new framework leads to a genuinely flexible approach or simply changes public relations rhetoric. For patients, delaying approval for another multi-year trial means losing irreversible physical independence.

New statistical method matches cellular states to known biological perturbations

Nature BiotechnologyBiotech & Programmable LifeProgrammable LifeSource published2026-11-10 08:00 SGT
Research paperStage not established

Single-cell perturbation dictionaries are vital tools for interpreting large-scale transcriptomic data by cataloguing how cells respond to various biological and chemical stimuli. However, matching new experimental cellular states to these reference dictionaries has historically been difficult due to technical noise and biological variability. To solve this, Grabski and colleagues introduced RNA fingerprinting, a two-stage statistical approach. First, it extracts denoised perturbation signatures from a reference dictionary. Next, it applies a Bayesian probabilistic framework—adapted from genome-wide association studies—to compare query cells against these fingerprints. According to ground-truth dataset evaluations, the method reliably identifies known perturbations across cell types and scales effectively to genome-wide datasets and combinatorial perturbations. This framework offers a robust solution for researchers analyzing complex single-cell transcriptomic responses.
