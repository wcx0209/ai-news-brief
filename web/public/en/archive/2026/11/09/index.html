HOPE: A Smarter Way to Trim Large AI Models Without Losing Brainpower

Background Large Language Models increasingly rely on Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures to scale up to hundreds of billions of parameters without burning excessive compute during inference. However, a major bottleneck is that every expert must be loaded into memory, even though only a tiny fraction are active for any given token. For instance, in models like Qwen3.5, expert layers consume roughly 95% of the total parameter memory footprint. Details To fix this, researchers use expert pruning to permanently delete the least useful experts. While older methods evaluate each expert independently, the creators of HOPE realized that experts form cooperative groups that activate together. HOPE introduces a second-order method utilizing a quadratic program to calculate pairwise expert interactions. This ensures that when an expert is removed, the interdependent cluster remains intact. Impact In tests across models like Qwen and GLM, HOPE excelled at aggressive pruning rates. At a 50% pruning rate, HOPE achieved top performance, matching the unpruned model's score on agentic coding benchmarks like SWE-Bench Pro, whereas traditional pruning methods suffered significant performance drops. This allows for massive memory savings, leaving room for longer contexts and deeper reasoning without hardware upgrades.

Forcing a Letter Suppresses Refusal: Mechanistic Evidence from WMDP

Safety benchmarks for large language models frequently rely on multiple-choice question (MCQ) formats, yet prior work shows this format suppresses model refusal. This preprint provides the first mechanistic explanation of this phenomenon using Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen3-8B on the Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy (WMDP) dataset. Using the tuned lens technique, the researchers demonstrate that when processing MCQs, models commit to an answer-letter token very early, contrasting with the gradual build-up of refusal seen in open-ended formats. Furthermore, activations during MCQ processing lie approximately three times further from the internal refusal direction. Ablating attention heads that carry the MCQ answer increases refusal rates, directly evidencing that answer-selection competes with and overrides safety refusal mechanisms. This implies that MCQ safety benchmarks often measure raw knowledge capabilities rather than true alignment or safety posture. As a preprint, code release and independent replication are necessary to fully corroborate these internal activation claims.

MIT's new platform helps robots understand hidden meanings in human commands

Background Humans naturally grasp intuitive meanings and unstated intentions behind words, but robots typically require precise, step-by-step mathematical instructions. To bridge this gap, researchers at MIT and New York University developed FLEX, a flexible feature-editing pipeline designed to help robots interpret natural language more like humans do. Details FLEX operates through a three-step process: Add, Generalize, and Mask. First, the robot adds specific objects mentioned by the user to its avoidance list. Second, it uses a large language model, specifically GPT-4o, to extract broader patterns from internet training data, deducing general rules such as avoiding fragile items or electronics without requiring explicit commands for every single item. Finally, it uses computer vision to update the environment and remove irrelevant items. Impact This technology reduces the heavy communication burden on human users, making household and assistive robots significantly easier to operate. Instead of meticulously programming safety rules, users can rely on natural conversations, paving the way for robots that adapt seamlessly to diverse home environments.

LingBot-Touch: Learning Heterogeneous Tactile Representation for General Dexterous Manipulation

Tactile sensing is essential for contact-rich dexterous manipulation, but existing commercial tactile sensors vary wildly in physics and design, making cross-sensor data scaling difficult. To address this, the researchers developed LingBot-Touch, a tactile foundation model designed to learn a unified and extensible representation across heterogeneous tactile sensors including piezoresistive, magnetic, and visuotactile devices. The model outputs plug-and-play tactile tokens that integrate seamlessly into existing policy architectures, such as diffusion policies and vision-language-action (VLA) models like pi_0.5 and LingBot-VLA. Extensive experiments spanning simulation and real-robot deployments across multiple hardware platforms show that the proposed representation consistently improves manipulation dexterity while maintaining high computational efficiency. As a preprint, further independent replication and code or weight releases will help confirm the generalizability of these cross-sensor tactile representations.
