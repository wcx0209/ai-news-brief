Historical brief · 2026-11-18

The grid will fall short of AI’s power needs. These 6 resources can close the gap.

According to Terrestrial Energy CEO Simon Irish, the U.S. power grid faces a severe shortfall driven by AI data centers, with McKinsey estimating an extra 30 GW to 55 GW needed by 2030.

The Power Shortage Reality

As artificial intelligence and data center demand soar, prominent energy voices warn that traditional power grids will fall short. According to McKinsey & Company, the U.S. could need an additional 30 GW to 55 GW of capacity by 2030. Furthermore, market reality is already biting: PJM Interconnection, the largest U.S. grid operator, recently held a capacity auction that fell 6.6 GW short of reliability targets, clearing at the price cap. Terrestrial Energy CEO Simon Irish argues that instead of debating demand forecasts, stakeholders must assume a shortfall and actively deploy solutions.

Six Solutions and Their Limits

Irish outlines six resources to bridge the gap, noting that no single option is fast or large enough on its own. Flexible demand is the fastest since it requires no new generation, though data centers are often reluctant to curtail AI workloads. Existing nuclear plants offer reliable power through uprates and restarts, but opportunities are running thin. Natural gas provides firm power, but equipment backlogs mean heavy-duty turbines ordered today may not arrive until 2031. Wind, solar, and storage deploy quickly, but require massive overbuilding to match round-the-clock data center needs. On-site generation avoids grid delays, yet still suffers from equipment shortages. Finally, advanced nuclear energy offers clean, firm power at scale, though it will not close the gap within this decade.

Strategic Principles

To successfully navigate this energy crisis, utilities and developers must match each solution to its specific timeline, use flexibility as a contract requirement for early grid access, and secure long-lead items for clean firm power early. Strategic planning must avoid irreversible over-commitments while ensuring that infrastructure built today remains valuable as a long-term asset for future reliability.
