Transient beta oscillations precede voluntary movement stopping in rats

Researchers have discovered that a single beta wave cycle occurs in the motor cortex about 200 milliseconds before a rat voluntarily stops running. Previous studies struggled to pin down this timing because traditional tests had hidden stopping times.

Nature Brain-Machine Interfaces ↗Source published 2026-11-14 08:00 SGTResearch paperNeurotechnology & Human Interfaces

Background

Brain wave activity known as beta oscillations has long been linked to how we control and inhibit movement, but scientists have struggled to pin down their exact timing relative to when a movement actually stops. This confusion largely stems from traditional testing methods where the exact moment of stopping is hidden or covert.

Details

To clear this up, researchers recorded 32-electrode brain activity alongside the running speed of head-fixed rats as they stopped running on a treadmill. They observed that a single beta wave cycle reliably occurs in the left motor cortex about 200 milliseconds before the rat stops. Using information theory, the study shows that beta power holds clues about future speed, but only when the animal is actively planning to stop. When researchers artificially added small amounts of time jitter to mimic older testing methods, this clear brain-action link completely broke down.

Impact

Using machine learning, the team demonstrated that despite being a clear biological marker of stopping, this specific beta oscillation has limited practical use for real-time decoding of actions. This work proposes a fresh conceptual model for how the brain controls the physical stopping of actions.
