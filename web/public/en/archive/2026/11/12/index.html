NVIDIA/auto-ontology: A Python framework for building semantic, graph-based reasoning layers

Detailed Features Auto Ontology acts as a bridge between your raw databases and AI agents. It automatically ingests database schemas, profiles columns, and infers table relationships to build a semantic graph of your enterprise data. Developers can use it to translate natural language questions into accurate SQL queries powered by NVIDIA NIM, ensuring that every answer comes with verifiable provenance and execution steps. System Architecture The project is designed with a modern microservices architecture. It includes a Next.js frontend for the Web UI, a FastAPI Python backend for chat and catalog management, an ingestion worker for vector embeddings, and a PostgreSQL database equipped with pgvector to store both application data and the semantic ontology. Installation and Usage Getting started is straightforward using Docker Compose. You simply need to clone the repository, configure your environment variables including your NVIDIA API key in a .env file, and run Docker Compose to spin up the local stack. Once running, you can connect your databases and start asking questions through the built-in chat interface or integrate it with other tools via the MCP server.

Google Research uses wearables and AI to forecast personalized health habits

Background Heart disease, kidney conditions, and metabolic disorders like type 2 diabetes form an interconnected web known as cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome. These issues often develop silently years before symptoms appear, and while smartwatches easily track past activity, translating that data into meaningful, actionable guidance remains a major challenge. Framework Details Google's new framework introduces a coachable health age model, which avoids simply guessing a user's birth year. Instead, it relies on published epidemiological studies linking behaviors like resting heart rate, steps, and sleep to disease risk. It is paired with Sensor World Model, an action-conditioned model pre-trained on millions of user-weeks of data to forecast how 20 physiological markers will evolve over 12 weeks under different daily routines. Impact Evaluated across multiple cohorts and through blind tests with medical professionals, this multimodal approach combines continuous wearable data with routine blood biomarkers to map multi-organ aging. While currently an investigational research prototype, it lays the groundwork for future prospective trials aimed at helping people build lasting, healthy habits.

Google launches Gemini Decisions API for fast, low-cost multimodal choices

How it works Instead of generating answer text token by token like traditional language models, Gemini Decision 1 scores candidate options in a single forward pass. It supports text, images, audio, video, and PDFs up to 256K tokens, allowing developers to ask up to 1,000 independent questions simultaneously. Every answer includes a confidence score, letting developers route ambiguous cases to fallback systems or human reviewers. Pricing and Performance The model delivers a median latency of 150-250 ms end-to-end and is priced at $0.10 per 1M input tokens with zero output tokens billed. Google reports that it is nearly 5x cheaper than standard generative alternatives across shared benchmark suites. Real-World Applications Internal teams at Google are already using the API as a low-latency control plane. Examples include dynamic model routing in Gemini Notebook to reduce query latency by up to 40%, rapid design iteration in Stitch, instant emoji selection in Google Labs, and safety layers for Model Context Protocol tools in Google Search.

Can AI Foundation Models Actually Explain How the Brain Works?

The Brain Data Rush Researchers are applying the recipe behind large language models to neuroscience, training neural networks on vast brain recordings. Meta, Stanford, and startups have released or are building brain foundation models, hoping that scale will make them smarter. The Great Debate Optimists argue that models reproducing brain activity can act as stand-ins for living subjects and help us discover new biological insights. Skeptics counter that fitting brain data does not guarantee the model works like a real brain, nor does it grant mechanistic understanding. Scaling laws for brain data also remain murky compared to language models. The Path Forward Some suggest combining data-driven models with task-trained models, while others argue that true intelligence requires modeling the whole body and physical interactions, reminding the field that scaling data might not be a silver bullet.

The Rapid Rise of AI in Food R&D A new report by agritech consultancy Bright Green Partners reveals that roughly 90% of food and beverage R&D leaders routinely use artificial intelligence. MIT and Northeastern engineers design stronger, more resilient metamaterials inspired by nature

Background For decades, synthetic metamaterials have been designed using a single building block, or unit cell, copied and pasted to form a precise, periodic microstructure. While this ordered approach improves overall performance, it has a major flaw: if one region cracks, the surrounding identical cells quickly buckle, leading to catastrophic failure. Details To overcome this limitation, a team from MIT and Northeastern University turned to network science, a field traditionally used to study complex systems like the internet or neural connections. Using a specialized algorithm, the researchers generated a physical network of intersecting beams with varying sizes and thicknesses, mimicking the web-like structures found in nature. By 3D-printing these patterns into polymer samples, they created scale-rich metamaterials that avoid the localization effect seen in ordered structures. Impact Physical tests showed that these network-inspired materials offer a widely controllable range of stiffness, resist sudden impacts more effectively, and can successfully cushion fragile objects. This design framework opens the door to next-generation protective gear, impact-absorbing structures, and specialized materials that control vibrations.

Is Claude Fable 5.2 worth it for office workers? Comparing it with Opus and Sonnet

The article evaluates Anthropic's new flagship AI model, Claude Fable 5.2, against Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5 across two typical office tasks: summarizing a 268-page government white paper into a 15-minute presentation, and conducting background research for a media interview. In the presentation-making task, Fable 5.2 followed the time constraint best, structuring the slides neatly with well-thought-out actionable takeaways and speaker notes. However, it took about 1.6 times longer and cost roughly twice as much as Opus 5.5, and 3.6 times as much as Sonnet 5.5. In the research task, all three models performed similarly well in grasping key contexts, though Fable 5.2 provided deeper localized insights at a higher cost. Third-party benchmarks indicate that Fable 5.2 leads in general intelligence but slightly trails in specific knowledge-work tasks like briefcase handling. Ultimately, while Fable 5.2 offers a more human-like output and better intent-matching for creative presentation structuring, its high cost means office workers should primarily rely on Opus 5.5 or Sonnet 5.5 for daily routines, reserving Fable 5.2 for high-stakes, heavy-duty information processing.
